# urwort: Grimm frame, history and culture research

Research for the two-layer concept. The frame layer: a fictional young assistant to the Brothers Grimm in Hesse, c. 1807 to 1815. The tale layer: playable worlds inside the tales. Compiled 2026-10-31.

## Confidence tags

- **[V]** I checked it this session against a cited source. Bracketed numbers like [3] point to the source list at the end.
- **[S]** Standard reference knowledge (common to biographies and encyclopedias) that I did not re-check this session. It is probably right, but check it before it goes into final dialogue.
- **[U]** Unverified, contested, anecdotal, or my inference. Do not present it as fact in the game without a primary source.

Where my sources disagree, I note it inline.

## 1. The frame setting: Kassel and Hesse, 1807-1815

### 1.1 Political timeline (the backdrop the player lives through)

**Gameplay note.** The frame's arc writes itself: occupation, a French-speaking court, conscription, the disaster of 1812, liberation and restoration, then hunger in 1816. Every chapter can move the political "weather" without any lecture.

### 1.2 Daily life in Westphalian Kassel

- **Language.** French was the language of the court and the upper administration. Jérôme knew so little German that Kassel people called him "König Lustik" after his supposed only phrase, "Morgen wieder lustik" [V] [22]. Laws and notices came out in French and German [S]. This gives a natural in-world reason for a learner to meet two languages and for German to feel like the language of "the people" (the Romantic idea, which the game can also question).
- **Administration.** The kingdom was divided into French-style départements; Kassel was in the Département de la Fulda [S]. There was civil marriage and civil registry under the Code Napoléon [S]. Accounts were kept in francs while older coin (Taler, Groschen, Heller) still circulated [S]. Jacob's pay was quoted in francs (see 1.3) [V].
- **Burdens.** Heavy war contributions, taxes and conscription led to bankruptcy by 1812 [V] [7]. Expect conscription notices, families hiding sons, and wounded or discharged soldiers on the roads (that last is my inference [U], but it fits the many soldier tales the Grimms recorded).
- **Reforms with real winners.** Jews got civil equality, serfdom and guild compulsion ended, and Kassel got a Jewish consistory and school reforms [V] [7][20]. When Westphalia fell in 1813, many of these gains were rolled back, and the consistory closed in 1813 [V] [20]. This is important for the sensitivity layer (section 4): the frame can show Jewish neighbours as real people at the very moment the Grimms were recording anti-Jewish tales.
- **Court glamour.** Jérôme renamed Schloss Wilhelmshöhe "Napoleonshöhe" and ran a lavish French-style court [V] [22]. The Kulturstiftung des Bundes funded a 2008 Kassel exhibition titled "König Lustik!?" that re-examined Jérôme's reputation [V] [22]. It is a good reference for not making him a cartoon.
- **Art looting.** French commissioners took paintings from the Kassel collections to Paris [S]. Some ended up at Joséphine's Malmaison and later in the Hermitage [U]. In 1814-15 Jacob was sent to recover Hessian cultural property from France [V] [6]. This makes a ready-made quest line.

### 1.3 The Grimms' lives, 1805-1815

- **Origins.** Jacob was born 4 Jan 1785 and Wilhelm 24 Feb 1786, both in Hanau [V] [5][6]. Their childhood was in Steinau an der Straße, where the father was Amtmann and the family lived in the Amtshaus. The father died in 1796 and the family fell into poverty [S]. An aunt at the Kassel court, Henriette Zimmer, paid for their schooling at the Lyceum Fridericianum in Kassel from 1798 [S for the aunt; V for the schooling [5][6]].
- **Marburg (1802-1806).** Both brothers studied law at Marburg under Friedrich Carl von Savigny, who opened Romantic circles and old manuscripts to them [V] [5][6]. In 1805 Jacob went to Paris to help Savigny with archive research [V] [6].
- **Household in Kassel.** From 1805 the mother and younger siblings lived in Kassel. The house was in the Marktgasse (No. 17 per one source; it no longer stands) [V] [24]. Grimmwelt gives "Am Sack" alley near the palace for 1798-1814 [V] [9]. The two may be the same area under different names [U], so check before placing it on a map. The Wild family's Sonnenapotheke was at Marktgasse 21, "only two houses from the first residence of the Brothers Grimm" [V] [12]. Siblings: Carl, Ferdinand, Ludwig Emil (a painter and etcher) and Lotte (Charlotte, 1793-1833), who kept house [S]; for Lotte's dates see [13]. The mother died in May 1808 [V] [10].
- **Money and posts.**
  - In 1806 Jacob was a clerk at the Hessian War College secretariat. In 1807/08 he served on the provisions commission for occupation troops, but he resigned [V] [6].
  - From 1808 to 1813 he was director of Jérôme's private library at Wilhelmshöhe (Napoleonshöhe) [V] [6][4]. His pay was 2,000 francs, rising to 4,000 when he also became an Auditeur au Conseil d'État from 1809. His duties were "nominal", which left time for research [V] [4].
  - Wilhelm had no paid post until 1814, when he became secretary of the Kassel library [V] [5]. This is a strong character note: Jacob's French salary supported the household and the research.
- **Wilhelm's health.** He had heart trouble and restlessness. In 1809 he went to Halle for treatment by the physician Johann Christian Reil (Goethe was also one of Reil's spa guests), and he met Goethe in Weimar on the way home [V] [5][25].
- **Work.**
  - From 1806 they collected anonymously for Arnim and Brentano's *Des Knaben Wunderhorn* [V] [5].
  - Wilhelm published Danish heroic songs and ballads in 1811 [V] [5].
  - KHM volume 1 came out on 20 Dec 1812 in Berlin: 86 tales, 900 copies, from the Realschulbuchhandlung of Georg Andreas Reimer [V] [5][26].
  - Volume 2 is dated 1815 but appeared at the end of 1814: about 70 more tales, 1,100 copies [V] [5][27].
  - *Altdeutsche Wälder* ran from 1813 to 1816 [V] [5]. *Deutsche Sagen* appeared in 1816 and 1818 [V] [6].
  - Volume 1 is dedicated to Bettina von Arnim for her son Johannes Freimund [S]. Achim and Bettina von Arnim visited the Grimms in Kassel in 1811 and saw the collection [V] [26].
- **After the liberation.** Jacob became legation secretary to the Hessian envoy from 1813 to 1815. He went to the Congress of Vienna in 1814, where he hunted manuscripts in Viennese libraries, and to Paris in 1815 as a commissioner for restitution [V] [6]. From 1814 Jacob, Wilhelm and Lotte lived in the Torwache on the Wilhelmshöher Allee [V] [24][9].
- **Later.** Göttingen 1830 to 1837, the Göttingen Seven in 1837, and Berlin from 1840/41 [V] [6] (see section 3).

### 1.4 The storytellers: NPC biographies

**Dorothea Viehmann (1755-1815)**, the anchor NPC.
- Born Katharina Dorothea Pierson on 8 Nov 1755 in Rengershausen (now Baunatal). She grew up in her father's inn, the Knallhütte, which still stands, and heard tales from the travellers who stopped there [V] [1][2].
- Her paternal ancestors were Huguenot refugees and she could speak and write French [V] [1].
- She married the tailor Nikolaus Viehmann in 1777 and lived in Niederzwehren from 1787. They had seven children, two of whom died young [V] [2].
- She met the Grimms in 1813 and gave "over 40" tales and variants, most of them in volume 2 [V] [1][2].
- The Grimms' 1815 preface calls her a "Bäuerin" (peasant woman) from "Zwehrn" who was "not much over fifty". In fact she was a middle-class tailor's wife of about 58 [V] [27][3]. This is a good, subtle example of how the Grimms shaped the image of the "folk" informant.
- Ludwig Emil Grimm drew and etched her portrait. An etching was rediscovered in 2012 [V] [2].
- She died on 17 Nov 1815, poor and widowed (widowhood [S]).
- In 2023 Holger Ehrhardt found 54 further manuscript drafts in the Berlin Grimm estate. Some are Viehmann transcripts that keep her dialect and her "X and X" doublings, and they re-attribute one tale from the Haxthausens to her [V] [28].
- Attributed tales (KHM numbers per [2]): 6, 9, 29, 34, 61, 63, 71, 76, 89, 94, 98, 100, 102, 106, 108, 111, 115, 118, 125, 127, 128 [V]. **Note that KHM 115 (*Die klare Sonne bringt's an den Tag*) is one of the three antisemitic KHM tales** (section 4).

**The Hassenpflugs (Kassel; Huguenot mother from the Dauphiné)** [V] [11][13]
- The father, Johannes Hassenpflug (1755-1834), was a senior official [V] [13].
- **Marie** (1788-1856) married von Dalwigk in 1814. Her tales: *Dornröschen* (50), *Rotkäppchen* (26), *Das Mädchen ohne Hände* (31), *Der Räuberbräutigam* (40), *Brüderchen und Schwesterchen* (11), and probably *Schneewittchen* (53), about 12 in all [V] [11][13].
- **Jeannette/Johanna** (1791-1860) gave about 10, including *Rotkäppchen*, *Die drei Spinnerinnen* (14) and *Tischchen deck dich* (36) [V] [13].
- **Amalie** (1800-1871) gave *Die drei Männlein im Walde* (13) and *Der Herr Gevatter* (42) [V] [13].
- In 1975/1986 Heinz Rölleke showed that "die alte Marie", long imagined as an old nursemaid in the Wild household, was most likely the young Marie Hassenpflug. Hermann Rebel contests this [V] [11]. This is great meta-material: the myth of the peasant grandmother storyteller as against the reality of educated young women with French tale-books.
- Their brother **Ludwig Hassenpflug** (1794-1862) married Lotte Grimm in 1822 [S], and Lotte died in 1833 [V] [13]. He later became the arch-conservative minister of Hesse-Kassel, nicknamed "Hessen-Haß-pflug" [S], so the young idealist in the frame becomes a reactionary. This is a long-arc irony to use.

**The Wilds (Sonnenapotheke, Marktgasse 21)** [V] [12]
- The father, Rudolf Wild, was an apothecary [S for the name]. The mother, Dorothea Catharina (1752-1813), gave 2 tales [V].
- **Dortchen** (Henriette Dorothea, 1793-1867) gave or co-gave about 15 tales, including *Frau Holle* (24) and *Rumpelstilzchen* (55) [V] [12]. Other sources also credit her with *Hänsel und Gretel* (15), *Allerleirauh* (65), *König Drosselbart* (52), *Der Froschkönig* (1, Wild family), *Die Wichtelmänner* (39) and *Die sechs Schwäne* [S/U] [29]. She married Wilhelm on 15 May 1825 [V] [5].
- Her sisters: **Gretchen** (Margarete Marianne, 1787-1819) gave 6 tales, **Lisette** (Johanna Elisabeth, 1782-1858) gave 2 jointly with Dortchen, and **Marie Elisabeth** (1794-1858) gave 1 [V] [12].
- The popular claim that Dortchen's father disapproved and she met Wilhelm "in secret" comes from Kate Forsyth's novel *The Wild Girl* [U] [29]. Treat it as fiction.

**The Haxthausens and Bökendorf (Bökerhof, near Brakel, Paderborn region; Westphalian Low German area)**
- **August von Haxthausen** (1792-1866) was born in Bökendorf. He met Jacob in Göttingen (the date there is [U]) and collected tales and songs [V] [14]. Later he became a famous agrarian scholar; his study of the Russian village commune (obshchina) influenced the debates before the 1861 emancipation of the serfs [V] [14].
- Werner von Haxthausen and their sisters, including **Ludowine**, were sources too [V] [15].
- Their nieces **Jenny** and **Annette von Droste-Hülshoff** met Wilhelm in 1813 at Bökendorf [V] [15]. Jenny gave *Die zertanzten Schuhe* (133, published 1815) [V] [3].
- Annette later wrote *Die Judenbuche* (1842), set in this very region. That is a literary bridge for the sensitivity layer [S].
- *Die Bremer Stadtmusikanten* is credited to "two tales from the Paderborn region". The Haxthausens are "usually assumed" to be the go-between, "without proof" [V] [16]. The KHM 27 Wikipedia page claims Viehmann; the German article says she gave only a gentler variant [V] [16], so treat the English claim as unreliable.

**Philipp Otto Runge (1777-1810)**, a Romantic painter from Wolgast in Swedish Pomerania.
- He wrote down *Von dem Fischer un syner Fru* (19) and *Von dem Machandelboom* (47) in Pomeranian Low German in 1806 [V] [17][3].
- He died in 1810 before the KHM appeared [S]. In the game he can be a voice in letters, since he never met the player.

**Johann Friedrich Krause**, a retired dragoon sergeant from Hoof near Kassel and the "oldest contributor", from a poor background [V] [3][30].
- He gave *Der alte Sultan* (48), about an old dog thrown out after a life of service [V] [30].
- The anecdote that the Grimms paid him for tales with their old trousers is widely repeated [U].
- He is perfect for the veteran and old-age theme.

**Elisabeth Schellenberg, "the Marburg fairy-tale woman"** (died 1814)
- She was a poor woman in the Marburg hospice (St. Jost per [31]; often called the Elisabeth hospital [S]).
- In 1810 she was 64 and refused to tell Wilhelm her tales. He sent a woman and her children to visit her, and she then told *Aschenputtel* (21) and *Der goldene Vogel* (57) [V] [31].
- Holger Ehrhardt identified her by name, as reported in 2016 [V] [31].

**Others, all [S] and needing a check:**
- Ferdinand Siebert, a teacher in Treysa.
- Friederike Mannel, a pastor's daughter in Allendorf (credited with *Der süße Brei*? [U]).
- The Ramus sisters, daughters of the French Reformed pastor in Kassel.
- Jacob and Wilhelm's own sister Lotte.

### 1.5 Savigny, Brentano, Arnim and Romanticism

- **Savigny** (later the leader of the Historical School of law) was the brothers' teacher. He was married to Kunigunde Brentano, Clemens's sister [S].
- **Clemens Brentano** and **Achim von Arnim** published *Des Knaben Wunderhorn* (1805-1808). It was a nationalist-Romantic songbook freely "improved" by the editors, and the Grimms contributed to it [V] [5]. Brentano planned a tale collection of his own, and the Grimms sent him their material in 1810 [V] (see 1.6).
- **Arnim** married Bettina Brentano in 1811 [S]. He encouraged the Grimms to publish the tales themselves and helped arrange Reimer [S/V] [26].
- The Romantic ideas behind the project:
  - Folk poetry (Naturpoesie) against literary art poetry (Kunstpoesie) [S].
  - Language as the soul of a Volk [S].
  - The forest as a German space of mystery and refuge: Tieck's "Waldeinsamkeit" (1797) [S], and Caspar David Friedrich's *Der Chasseur im Walde* (1814), a lone French dragoon swallowed by a German forest [S].
- Jacob insisted on faithfulness to sources. Wilhelm, especially from 1819, created the polished "Grimm style" [V] [5]. This difference matches the game's two layers well: the player records *what was said*, and the Wilhelm NPC edits.

### 1.6 The Ölenberg manuscript story

- In autumn 1810 the Grimms copied their collected tales and sent the drafts to Brentano. Dates given are 17 Oct [32], 25 Oct and 15 Dec 1810 [33]. Sources differ, so the 1810 shipments were probably in two batches [U].
- Brentano never used them and never returned them [V] [8].
- The Grimms later destroyed their own drafts, probably so that the polished print could not be compared with them [V per a publisher's summary, 33; motive U].
- Brentano's papers reached the Trappist abbey of Ölenberg (Oelenberg) in Alsace. The manuscript was found in the early 1920s and edited by Joseph Lefftz (Heidelberg, 1927) [V] [33].
  - The English Wikipedia says "1970", which conflicts with the 1927 edition. Prefer the 1920s.
- It was later sold, with an auction in New York in 1953 reported by one source [U] [8-search]. It is now in the Fondation Martin Bodmer, Cologny, near Geneva [V] [8].
- Heinz Rölleke's critical edition came out in 1975 [V] [33]. It holds 46 manuscript items or 53/54 tales depending on how they are counted [V/U] [8][28].
- Ehrhardt's 2023 Berlin find of 54 more drafts [V] [28] shows that "lost manuscripts" is still live scholarship.

**Game hook.** The "lost bundle sent to Brentano" is a ready-made MacGuffin. The player is the assistant who copied the 1810 fair copies, so the player's own handwriting is in the bundle that vanishes. A late-game or epilogue reveal can show that it survived in an Alsatian monastery a century later. The fiction stays true to history: it never claims the player changed anything, only that they were there.

**Travel** [S/U]. Most travel was on foot or by post coach (Postkutsche), with carters' wagons for goods. Kassel to Marburg is about 90 km (2 to 3 days on foot or a long coach day). Kassel to Bökendorf is about 60 km. Kassel to Hanau and Steinau is about 150 to 170 km. Passports and Westphalian police checks at borders are plausible [U].

### 1.8 Candidate story beats (grounded)

1. **1807, arrival.** The player arrives in Kassel as a scholarship pupil or orphan, taken on as copyist by Jacob, who has just resigned from the provisions commission [V]. The player sees French notices on the town walls. *Learning:* first German words come from street signs that are paired with French.
2. **1808, mourning and salary.** The mother dies [V], and Jacob takes the library post at Napoleonshöhe [V]. The player runs errands between the Marktgasse and the court library. *Theme:* serving a foreign king to feed a German family is a dilemma, not a villainy.
3. **1809, Dörnberg's rising** [V]. Rumours, peasants marching, a fast collapse. Wilhelm leaves for Halle [V]. *Choice:* the player can help hide a neighbour's son from the conscription lists [U, fictional but plausible].
4. **1810, the Marburg go-between** [V]. Schellenberg refuses Wilhelm, so he sends a woman with children. The player, a child or youth, comes along and wins her trust. The reward is *Aschenputtel* and *Der goldene Vogel*.
5. **Oct 1810, the Brentano bundle** [V]. The player copies the fair copies and they leave for Brentano. Seed the Ölenberg epilogue here.
6. **1811, visitors and fire.** The Arnims visit [V], and the palace burns on 24 Nov [V]. *Event:* the player rescues pages, or watches the court move to Bellevue.
7. **1812, the year of absence.** The army marches to Russia and almost no one comes back [V]. Volume 1 appears in December [V]. *Tone:* the book of children's tales is born in a year of dead sons. Krause the veteran tells *Der alte Sultan*.
8. **1813, Viehmann.** The meeting [V]: she tells slowly so they can write, and her dialect makes a natural listening and dictation mechanic. In late September the Cossack raid [V]. In October Leipzig and Hanau, and Jérôme flees. In November the Elector returns [S]. The Droste sisters at Bökendorf [V].
9. **1814, restoration.** The family moves to the Torwache [V]. Jacob goes to Paris and Vienna to recover looted manuscripts [V]; a quest is to track a missing Kassel codex. Marie Hassenpflug marries [V]. Volume 2 is printed.
10. **1815, endings.** Viehmann dies on 17 Nov [V], a quiet goodbye scene with the Ludwig Emil portrait. Tambora erupts far away. **Coda, 1816:** the "year without a summer" and hunger [V], and *Hänsel und Gretel* stops being a fairy tale.

**Other NPCs grounded in fact:** Ludwig Emil Grimm (the artist brother, who sketches the player [U]); Lotte Grimm (who runs the house); Israel Jacobson or a member of the Kassel consistory (counter-voice, section 4); a Westphalian gendarme; a French clerk at the library (the player's French-speaking ally); Bettina von Arnim (a force of nature, in letters and on a visit).

## 2. Tale-by-tale culture and history map

Edition and number key: KHM numbers are from the final 1857 edition. "1812" means volume 1 of the first edition, "1815" volume 2, and "1819+" a tale added or reshaped later. Source attributions are tagged. Where no tag appears, the attribution follows the standard Rölleke attributions as summarised in [2][11][12][13]. Culture points marked *(inf.)* are my design inferences: plausible teaching links, not claims about the tale's origin.

## 3. Broader German history and culture threads (long arc)

Each thread has a note on how it could surface in play without lecturing.

1. **End of the Holy Roman Empire (1806) and the patchwork of states.**
   - *Surface:* currencies and border posts change between chapters (Taler, Gulden, francs). In *Fischer un syner Fru* the Kaiser rung of the ladder is "empty" in 1806, and an NPC notices that wryly.
   - *Mechanic:* a coin pouch with several currencies, so exchange-rate talk teaches number words.
2. **The Napoleonic era: occupation, reform and resistance (1806-1815).**
   - *Surface:* the Code Napoléon gives civil marriage and Jewish equality, while conscription and taxes press down. Players see both the gains and the burdens, and avoid a simple "French bad, Germans good" story (historically accurate and respectful).
   - *Mechanic:* bilingual French/German documents; a dialogue choice for each policy notice.
3. **Romanticism, the forest and the idea of the Volk.**
   - *Surface:* Tieck's *Waldeinsamkeit*, C. D. Friedrich's chasseur painting, the Wunderhorn. The forest is both a hunger margin (woodcutters, *Hutewald*) and a Romantic symbol. Let the player feel both: "the gentlemen in Kassel love the forest; the woodcutter's wife fears it."
   - *Mechanic:* the forest is a recurring hub between tale worlds, and its mood shifts with whoever is narrating.
4. **Dialects and the High German consonant shift.**
   - *Surface:* travelling from Kassel north to Bökendorf crosses into Low German country. Runge's tales are in Pomeranian Low German. In the Benrath line *maken* becomes *machen*; the line is usually drawn passing near or north of Kassel [U, check on a map]. Low German *Water, Appel, Pann, Tid* match English *water, apple, pan, tide*, while High German has *Wasser, Apfel, Pfanne, Zeit*.
   - *Mechanic:* this is exactly urwort's cognate system. NPCs from the north "speak English-ish", and the player learns the shift rules p→pf/f, t→z/s and k→ch as **spell transformations**.
   - Jacob formulated the sound-shift laws ("Grimm's law") in his *Deutsche Grammatik* (1819; the shift rules are in the 1822 2nd edition [S]; Rasmus Rask got there in parallel in 1818 [S]). The player can literally watch Jacob discover it.
5. **The Luther Bible and standard German.**
   - *Surface:* the one book in almost every house. Storytellers quote Luther's phrasing. Luther worked from the East Central German Saxon chancery language [S]. Written High German unites people who could barely understand one another's dialects.
   - *Mechanic:* "Bible-German" as a shared lingua franca in dialect regions; an NPC reading Luther aloud teaches the standard forms.
6. **Huguenots and migration.**
   - *Surface:* Viehmann, the Hassenpflugs and the Ramus family all have French Protestant roots. The tales the Grimms took as "German" were partly French. Migration is a constant thread, through the Huguenots, the Hameln/Transylvania legend, the 1816 emigrants to America and Russia [V] [23], and Haxthausen in Russia.
   - *Mechanic:* tracing a tale's family tree across languages (Perrault → Hassenpflug → Grimm) is a mini-etymology for stories.
7. **Wars of liberation (1813-15) and the birth of nationalism.**
   - *Surface:* Leipzig, Hanau, volunteers, patriotic poetry (Arndt, Körner [S]). Keep it ambivalent: liberation from occupation, but also the start of an exclusive nationalism.
8. **Restoration and the Biedermeier (1815-1848).**
   - *Surface:* the Elector restores old privileges [S]. Wilhelm's later editions turn biological mothers into stepmothers and add moral polish [V] [19]. Show it as a meta-mechanic: the same tale world "re-skins" from the raw 1812 version to the Biedermeier 1857 one.
9. **The Göttingen Seven (1837).**
   - On 18 Nov 1837 seven professors, including both Grimms, protested the King of Hanover's revocation of the constitution. They were dismissed on 12 Dec, and Jacob, Dahlmann and Gervinus were expelled [V] [35].
   - *Surface:* an epilogue letter from the now-grown assistant ("Your old master has been thrown out of Hanover for keeping his oath").
10. **The 1848 revolution and the Paulskirche.**
    - Jacob sat in the Frankfurt National Assembly. On 4 Jul 1848 he proposed a new first article, "Das deutsche Volk ist ein Volk von Freien, und deutscher Boden duldet keine Knechtschaft. Fremde Unfreie, die auf ihm verweilen, macht er frei." It was rejected by 192 votes to 205 [V] [36]. He also proposed abolishing legal distinctions between nobles, burghers and peasants [V] [36].
    - *Surface:* an epilogue; the line connects to the serfdom abolished in 1807.
11. **The Deutsches Wörterbuch (from 1838) and language as a national project.**
    - Begun in 1838; Jacob only reached "Frucht" [V] [6]. It was finished in 1961, a project that outlived the Grimms, two German states and the Nazi era [S].
    - *Mechanic:* urwort's dictionary or word-graph is the player's own "Wörterbuch". The end-game "Urworte" link to Jacob's etymological ambition.
12. **Reception, appropriation and post-war (meta layer).**
    - **Nazi era:** the tales went into school curricula and propaganda, and the unexpurgated antisemitic tales were used to indoctrinate [V] [18][37].
    - **After the war:** the claim that the Allies banned the KHM (often stated as a British-zone publication ban in 1946) is widely repeated but thinly sourced [U] [37]. Present it as "reports say" or not at all.
    - **The divided-Germany era:** GDR DEFA tale films (*Das singende, klingende Bäumchen*, 1957 [S]) and West German school use.
    - **Today:** the Deutsche Märchenstraße and UNESCO Memory of the World status for the Grimms' own copies of the KHM (2005 [S]).
    - *Surface:* a frame-breaking epilogue museum (like the GRIMMWELT in Kassel), where the player sees what later people did with the words they helped collect.
13. **Hunger and the potato.**
    - *Surface:* millet porridge in *Der süße Brei* and bread in *Hänsel* point back to the cereal age. The potato is spreading but a crop failure is still catastrophic. Then 1816 [V] [23].
    - *Mechanic:* food words as an economy system.

## 4. Sensitivities and recommendations

### 4.1 Antisemitic content
- **In the KHM:** *Der Jude im Dorn* (KHM 110), *Der gute Handel* (KHM 7) and *Die klare Sonne bringt's an den Tag* (KHM 115; its source was Viehmann) [V] [18][2]. Wilhelm's successive edits made the Jewish figure in KHM 110 more negative [V] [18]. Nazi educators used these tales [V] [18].
- **In *Deutsche Sagen*:** the blood-libel legends *Der Judenstein* and *Das von den Juden getötete Mägdlein* [V] [18].
- **Recommendations:**
  - **Never make these playable tale worlds**, and never put the stereotypes into an NPC's mouth as neutral flavour.
  - If they are acknowledged at all, do it through the **frame**: for example, the player hears a storyteller tell *Der Jude im Dorn* while a Jewish neighbour from the Kassel consistory (founded 1808 [V] [20]) stands nearby. The player can choose not to write it down, or can talk to the neighbour. This is accurate, because emancipation came in 1808 and was rolled back after 1813, and it gives Jewish characters full personhood.
  - Include **positive, historically grounded Jewish NPCs** (consistory members, a Seesen school teacher, a Jewish trader on the Kassel market) whose stories exist apart from the antisemitic tales.
  - Consider an optional, sober note in an in-game archive or museum layer (the epilogue) explaining the blood libel and the Nazi use, written with input from a Jewish educator or a museum source. Do **not** gamify it.
  - Be careful with the *Judenbuche* (Droste) reference and avoid "ambient" antisemitism as local colour.

### 4.2 Violence and abuse
- The 1812 tales include parental abandonment, cannibalism (*Machandelboom*, and in 1812 the queen wants to eat Snow White's lungs and liver [S]), mutilation (*Mädchen ohne Hände*, the toe and heel cutting), incestuous desire (*Allerleirauh*), and the children's slaughter-game tale (cut after 1812).
- **Recommendation:** keep the darkness, since it is historically important that 1812 is raw. Stage it with restraint: off-screen, symbolic or shadow-play, with content notes. Never make a child-harm scene interactive. Let the frame NPCs discuss why the tales are dark (hunger, war, mortality) rather than revel in gore.

### 4.3 Gender
- The storytellers were largely women, yet the "Grimm" brand erased them for a long time, and the preface turned Viehmann into a "peasant". The tales punish idle spinners and reward obedient girls.
- **Recommendation:** foreground the women storytellers as authors (they are the game's real "quest-givers"). Let the player see Wilhelm's edits change a woman's voice, so the critique emerges through mechanics.

### 4.4 Nationalist, colonial and *völkisch* readings
- **Nationalism.** The Grimms' project was part of building a national identity, and the Kyffhäuser legend, the forest and the "Volk" were later absorbed into exclusive and Nazi ideologies [V] [37].
  - **Recommendation:** present the Romantic nation idea in its 1813 context: liberal, anti-occupation, constitutional (Göttingen 1837, Paulskirche 1848 [V]). Let the epilogue show how it was turned exclusive, without retro-projecting Nazism onto the Grimms.
  - The French in the frame should be people (a clerk, Jérôme as more than a cartoon [V] [22]), not "the enemy".
- **Colonial era.** The KHM were translated and used in colonial-era German schools and missions, and racialised imagery appears in 19th- and 20th-century illustrated editions [U, general claim]. *Der Jude im Dorn*'s sister motifs and some illustrations use racial caricature.
  - **Recommendation:** source art from the 1812-1830s (Ludwig Emil Grimm's etchings) or create new art. Do not reuse late-19th-century illustrations without review.
- **Romani and other minorities.** Some Sagen and later editions include "Zigeuner" stereotypes [U]. Treat them the same way as 4.1.
- **"Germanic" pseudo-history.** Jacob's *Deutsche Mythologie* fed later *völkisch* mysticism [S].
  - **Recommendation:** in the Urworte layer, keep to the user's rule of rigorous Proto-Indo-European (PIE) or Proto-Germanic with two sources (see the urwort memory notes). Avoid runic or "Aryan" aesthetics and anything that echoes Nazi iconography (for example, specific rune forms used by the SS).

### 4.5 Real people
- The Grimms and the storytellers are real. Invent no romances, crimes or scandals for them.
  - The "secret meetings" of Dortchen and Wilhelm come from a novel [U] [29]. Their marriage in 1825 [V] is fine to foreshadow gently.
- Use the documented record of their words (the prefaces, Ölenberg texts, letters) where possible. Keep the fictional player's influence invisible in the historical record.
