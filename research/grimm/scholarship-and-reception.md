# The Grimms: scholarship, editions, and reception. An annotated bibliography for urwort

Compiled 2026-10-31 with web search and fetch. This is a secondary-literature guide, not original research.

**Verification key**

- **[verified]**: checked against a primary text (usually the Wikisource transcription of the relevant KHM edition) or an authoritative institutional page during this session.
- **[sourced]**: stated by a reliable secondary source cited here. The primary was not checked.
- **[unverified]**: often repeated, but no reliable source for it was found this session. Do not present it as fact in the game.
- **OA** means open access.

Abbreviations: KHM = *Kinder- und Hausmärchen*; DWB = *Deutsches Wörterbuch*; ATU = Aarne–Thompson–Uther tale-type index; DTA = Deutsches Textarchiv; EM = *Enzyklopädie des Märchens*.

## 0. Quick facts relevant to the game's setting (1807–1815)

- After Napoleon's victories, Hesse-Kassel was absorbed into the Kingdom of Westphalia (1807–1813) under Jérôme Bonaparte. In 1808 Jacob became librarian to King Jérôme. Wilhelm later remembered the period as one of "fremde Menschen, fremde Sitten … eine fremde, laut geredete Sprache". Jacob served as a Hessian legation secretary in Paris in 1814 and then went to the Congress of Vienna. **[sourced]** Bleek, APuZ 1986 (see §2).
- The manuscript the Grimms sent to Clemens Brentano in 1810 survived in the Trappist abbey of Ölenberg (Alsace). It is the earliest state of many tales. KHM vol. 1 appeared in December 1812 with 86 tales; vol. 2 followed in 1815 with 70 tales. The seventh edition (1857, the "Ausgabe letzter Hand") has 200 numbered tales plus 10 children's legends. **[sourced]**
- Dorothea Viehmann (1755–1815) of Niederzwehren, the "Märchenfrau" of the 1815 preface, died in 1815. That falls inside the game's timeframe.
- The Grimms' own annotated copies (Handexemplare) of the KHM are kept in Kassel. They were added to UNESCO's Memory of the World register in 2005. **[verified]** GRIMMWELT Kassel press release, 2025: https://www.grimmwelt.de/files/downloads/Presse/2025/GRIMMWELT_Kassel_Pressemitteilung_20_Jahre_UNESCO_Welterbe.pdf

## 1. Core scholarship on the KHM and the Grimms

### Heinz Rölleke (1936–2023)
The leading German Grimm philologist. His editions and source studies are the basis for most of what we know about the Grimms' informants and how the texts developed.

- **Rölleke (ed.), *Die älteste Märchensammlung der Brüder Grimm. Synopse der handschriftlichen Urfassung von 1810 und der Erstdrucke von 1812*.** Cologny-Genève: Fondation Martin Bodmer, 1975. The Ölenberg manuscript printed side by side with the 1812 texts. It shows how much the Grimms expanded and stylised the tales before they were first printed. **[sourced]**
- **Rölleke (ed.), *Kinder- und Hausmärchen. Ausgabe letzter Hand mit den Originalanmerkungen*.** 3 vols. Stuttgart: Reclam, 1980 and later reprints. The standard affordable critical text of the 1857 edition, with the Grimms' notes. **[sourced]**
- **Rölleke, "Die 'stockhessischen' Märchen der 'alten Marie'. Das Ende eines Mythos um die frühesten KHM-Aufzeichnungen der Brüder Grimm."** First published in German in the 1970s; the venue (GRM 1975) is **[unverified]**. Reprinted in his *Die Märchen der Brüder Grimm: Quellen und Studien* (Trier: WVT). English version: "The 'Utterly Hessian' Fairy Tales by 'Old Marie': The End of a Myth", in Ruth B. Bottigheimer (ed.), *Fairy Tales and Society: Illusion, Allusion, and Paradigm* (Philadelphia: U Pennsylvania Press, 1986), pp. 287–300. Herman Grimm had attributed key early tales to an old housekeeper, "die alte Marie" of the Wild family's pharmacy. Rölleke showed that the "Marie" in the Grimms' notes was the young, educated Marie Hassenpflug (b. 1788), from a family of Huguenot descent. This dismantled the image of an illiterate peasant source behind "Rotkäppchen", "Dornröschen" and other tales. **[sourced]** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Hassenpflug
- **Rölleke, *Die Märchen der Brüder Grimm. Eine Einführung*.** Munich/Zurich: Artemis, 1985, with later editions. **[sourced]**
- **Rölleke (ed.), *Es war einmal … Die wahren Märchen der Brüder Grimm und wer sie ihnen erzählte*.** Frankfurt: Eichborn, 2011, and later editions. Gives the informants' original versions. Review: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/504579

### Jack Zipes
Writes on the fairy tale as a social and historical form, with a Frankfurt School background.

- ***The Brothers Grimm: From Enchanted Forests to the Modern World*.** New York: Routledge, 1988; 2nd ed. Palgrave Macmillan, 2002. Argues that the tales were shaped to fit a bourgeois, Protestant, patriarchal ideal. Includes the chapter "The Struggle for the Grimms' Throne: The Legacy of the Grimms' Tales in the FRG and GDR since 1945". **[sourced]** https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-137-09873-3_10 (paywalled)
- ***The Complete Fairy Tales of the Brothers Grimm*.** New York: Bantam, 1987; later editions. A translation of the 1857 text plus tales that had been dropped.
- ***The Original Folk and Fairy Tales of the Brothers Grimm: The Complete First Edition*.** Princeton UP, 2014. ISBN 9780691160597. Illustrated by Andrea Dezsö. All 156 tales of 1812/15 in English. This is the best English way into the "un-sanitised" first edition. The publisher's claim that it is the "first" complete English translation of 1812/15 is commonly repeated; treat it as **[sourced]**, not independently checked. https://search.worldcat.org/oclc/879662315
- **"Breaking the Disney Spell"**, in E. Bell, L. Haas, L. Sells (eds.), *From Mouse to Mermaid* (Indiana UP, 1995). Critiques Disney's "Snow White" (see §4).
- ***Grimm Legacies: The Magic Spell of the Grimms' Folk and Fairy Tales*.** Princeton UP, 2015.

### Maria Tatar
- ***The Hard Facts of the Grimms' Fairy Tales*.** Princeton UP, 1987; 2nd expanded ed. 2003. Shows that across the editions Wilhelm removed sexual content but kept, and sometimes increased, violence, especially punitive violence against villains. Also reads the tales psychoanalytically and as social history. **[sourced]**
- ***The Annotated Brothers Grimm*.** New York: W. W. Norton, 2004; bicentennial ed. 2012. Annotated translations with notes on edition variants and reception, including Nazi-era use.

### Ruth B. Bottigheimer
- ***Grimms' Bad Girls and Bold Boys: The Moral and Social Vision of the Tales*.** Yale UP, 1987. Analyses speech, gender and silencing. Wilhelm's revisions took direct speech away from good girls and gave it to villains and men, and they reinforced gender norms. **[sourced]**
- **(ed.) *Fairy Tales and Society: Illusion, Allusion, and Paradigm*.** U Pennsylvania Press, 1986. Contains Rölleke's "Old Marie" essay.
- ***Fairy Tales: A New History*.** SUNY Press, 2009. A controversial thesis: the "rise" fairy tale came from print (Straparola) rather than oral tradition. Relevant to how much weight to give "folk" authenticity.

### John M. Ellis
- ***One Fairy Story Too Many: The Brothers Grimm and Their Tales*.** U Chicago Press, 1983. ISBN 9780226205465. A polemic. Ellis compares the Grimms with Macpherson's "Ossian" and argues they misrepresented their sources (middle-class, literate, partly of French/Huguenot background) and their editing in order to fabricate a German "folk" origin. Rölleke and others accept that the sources were misrepresented. Ellis's charge of deliberate *fraud*, and his description of Viehmann as essentially "middle-class and French", are widely regarded as overstated. Reviews: Indiana ScholarWorks https://scholarworks.iu.edu/dspace/items/9ee0a338-4523-4db0-88b9-582fd5a671d2 ; *International Fiction Review* https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/IFR/article/view/13721 (OA). Ellis also discusses the Nazi-era and postwar reception of the tales.

### Hans-Jörg Uther
- ***Handbuch zu den "Kinder- und Hausmärchen" der Brüder Grimm. Entstehung – Wirkung – Interpretation*.** Berlin: de Gruyter, 2008; 2nd ed. 2013. A tale-by-tale reference covering sources, edition history, ATU types, reception and literature. This is the first place to check any per-tale fact for the game. **[sourced]**
- ***The Types of International Folktales: A Classification and Bibliography*.** 3 vols. Folklore Fellows' Communications 284–286. Helsinki: Academia Scientiarum Fennica, 2004. The ATU index. It is copyrighted and has no open licence (see §6).
- Editor of the KHM for Diederichs/Diederichs Märchen der Weltliteratur; long-time editor at the *Enzyklopädie des Märchens*.

### Steffen Martus
- ***Die Brüder Grimm. Eine Biographie*.** Berlin: Rowohlt, 2009. Nominated for the Leipzig Book Fair Prize 2010. The best modern German dual biography. It presents the brothers as a working "research consortium" and an "inner unity of contrasts", and places them in Hessian, Westphalian and Prussian politics and in the period's media history. Reviews: https://www.perlentaucher.de/buch/steffen-martus/die-brueder-grimm.html

### Donald Haase
- **(ed.) *The Reception of Grimms' Fairy Tales: Responses, Reactions, Revisions*.** Detroit: Wayne State UP, 1993. Essential essays on reception, including the German postwar debate. **[sourced]**
- **(ed.) *Fairy Tales and Feminism: New Approaches*.** Wayne State UP, 2004.
- **(ed.) *The Greenwood Encyclopedia of Folktales and Fairy Tales*.** 3 vols. Greenwood, 2008.

### Christa Kamenetsky
- **"Folklore as a Political Tool in Nazi Germany."** *Journal of American Folklore* 85 (1972): 221–235. An OA copy is circulating: https://psi428.cankaya.edu.tr/uploads/files/Kamenetsky-FolklorePoliticalTool-1972.pdf
- **"Folktale and Ideology in the Third Reich."** *Journal of American Folklore* 90, no. 356 (1977): 168–178. Party-sponsored KHM editions; the "peasant" redefined as a fighter; the folktale used as a "bible" for indoctrinating youth and turned into a state product cut off from living tradition. **[sourced]**
- ***Children's Literature in Hitler's Germany: The Cultural Policy of National Socialism*.** Athens: Ohio UP, 1984; reissued 2019. ISBN 9780821446720. See ch. "Folktale, Germandom, and Race". Not OA. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/67980
- ***The Brothers Grimm and Their Critics: Folktales and the Quest for Meaning*.** Ohio UP, 1992. Surveys interpretive traditions, from Romantic and nationalist readings through the Nazi period to postwar and psychoanalytic ones.

### Others worth knowing
- **Kurt Ranke, Rolf Wilhelm Brednich et al. (eds.), *Enzyklopädie des Märchens*.** 15 vols. Berlin: de Gruyter, 1977–2015. The authoritative reference for tale types, motifs and scholars. Not OA.
- **Lutz Röhrich, *Märchen und Wirklichkeit*.** 1956; English *Folktales and Reality*, Indiana UP, 1991. How tales reflect historical realities.
- **Hermann Bausinger, "Kinder und Jugendliche im Spannungsfeld der Massenmedien – Die Wiederkehr des Märchens".** 1976. OA via Universität Tübingen: http://hdl.handle.net/10900/47615. Key on the postwar "Märchengreuel" campaign (§5).
- **Ulrike Bastian, *Die "Kinder- und Hausmärchen" der Brüder Grimm in der literaturpädagogischen Diskussion des 19. und 20. Jahrhunderts*.** Frankfurt: Haag + Herchen, 1981 (Studien zur Kinder- und Jugendmedienforschung 8). The standard study of the pedagogical debates over the tales, including Nazi-era and post-1945. Not OA.
- **Valerie Paradiz, *Clever Maids: The Secret History of the Grimm Fairy Tales*.** Basic Books, 2005. A popular account of the women informants.
- **Bruno Bettelheim, *The Uses of Enchantment*.** 1976. A psychoanalytic defence of the tales that helped end the postwar suspicion of them in West Germany. Contested, including allegations of plagiarism.
- **Martha B. Helfer, *The Word Unheard: Legacies of Anti-Semitism in German Literature and Culture*.** Evanston: Northwestern UP, 2011. Includes a chapter on Wilhelm's revisions of "Der Jude im Dorn" (§5).

## 2. The Grimms as linguists

### Grimm's law and the priority question
- **Rasmus Rask, *Undersøgelse om det gamle Nordiske eller Islandske Sprogs Oprindelse*.** Copenhagen, 1818; written c. 1814 for a prize competition. Rask first set out the regular consonant correspondences between Germanic and other Indo-European languages (e.g. Lat. *pater* : Goth. *fadar*). **[sourced]** Britannica, "Grimm's law": https://www.britannica.com/topic/Grimms-law. Text in W. P. Lehmann (ed.), *A Reader in Nineteenth-Century Historical Indo-European Linguistics* (Indiana UP, 1967), OA at UT Austin: https://lrc.la.utexas.edu/books/reader/3-r-rask
- **Jacob Grimm, *Deutsche Grammatik*, vol. 1.** Göttingen: Dieterich, 1819; 2nd, rewritten ed. 1822. The second edition systematised the first (Germanic) and second (High German) consonant shifts as the "Lautverschiebung", acknowledging Rask. Grimm's contribution was a systematic, historical presentation that included the High German shift. "Grimm's law" is a later English label. **[sourced]**
- **Karl Verner, "Eine Ausnahme der ersten Lautverschiebung."** *Zeitschrift für vergleichende Sprachforschung* 23 (1877). Verner's law explains the main exceptions. Relevant if the game's "sound law" puzzles want an "exception" mechanic.
- Further reading: Lehmann's *Reader* (above); Anna Morpurgo Davies, *Nineteenth-Century Linguistics* (History of Linguistics vol. IV, Longman, 1998).
- *Game note:* Grimm's law was formulated in 1819–1822, after the game's 1807–1815 window. In 1807–1815 Jacob was working on Old German poetry, the *Altdeutsche Wälder* (1813–16), and the KHM. Rask's work was unpublished until 1818. A character in 1810 could *notice* correspondences, but could not cite "Grimm's law".

### Deutsches Wörterbuch (DWB)
- The work began in 1838, after the brothers lost their Göttingen posts. The first fascicle appeared in 1852 and vol. 1 on 13 April 1854, with Jacob's long preface. Wilhelm edited D and died in 1859. Jacob edited A–C and E, and was working on F when he died on 20 September 1863; the entries were complete up to and including **"Frucht"**. The Prussian Academy took formal responsibility in 1908. After 1945 the work continued jointly in East Berlin and Göttingen. The final fascicle appeared in **January 1961**, giving 32 volumes (33 with the 1971 source list), about 320,000–330,000 headwords and about 67,000 columns. **[sourced]** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsches_W%C3%B6rterbuch ; Göttingen Academy project page: https://adw-goe.de/en/forschung/abgeschlossene-forschungsprojekte/akademienprogramm/deutsches-woerterbuch/
  - *Discrepancy:* the Göttingen Academy page summary gives "1960" for completion, while most sources give January 1961. Use "1961" or "1960/61".
  - A revised edition (2DWB, letters A–F) was produced by the Berlin and Göttingen academies and completed in 2016. **[sourced]** adw-goe.de, above.
- **Citation slips and volunteers.** The brothers relied on outside excerptors who read sources and sent in slips with quotations. A contemporary 1854 review counts **83 scholars** who contributed excerpts. **[sourced]** Search snippet from a Grenzboten review, not read in full: https://brema.suub.uni-bremen.de/grenzboten/download/pdf/159435. Jacob's 1854 *Vorrede* (vol. 1, cols. I–LXVIII) discusses the contributors and the method, and is the citable primary. It is digitised in the Wörterbuchnetz.
- Background: Alan Kirkness, *Geschichte des Deutschen Wörterbuchs 1838–1863* (Stuttgart: Hirzel, 1980), the standard history **[sourced]**; Ulrike Haß-Zumkehr's study of the DWB preface, which is OA: https://ids-pub.bsz-bw.de/frontdoor/deliver/index/docId/6704/file/Hass_Zumkehr_alle_welt_erwartet_hier_eine_erklaerung_von_mir_1997.pdf
- Digital: **Wörterbuchnetz (Universität Trier)**, https://woerterbuchnetz.de/DWB. A free online full text, double-keyed. Also searchable through DWDS. Licence for bulk data: **[unverified]**; ask Trier/TCDH.

### Politics: the Göttingen Seven (1837) and Frankfurt (1848)
- In 1837 King Ernst August of Hanover revoked the 1833 constitution. On 18 November 1837 (Bleek gives 17 November) seven Göttingen professors, among them Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm, Dahlmann and Gervinus, protested that their oath to the constitution still bound them. They were dismissed in December, and Jacob was expelled from the kingdom. Jacob justified himself in *Über meine Entlassung* (1838). **[sourced]**
- In 1846 Jacob chaired the first Germanistenversammlung in Frankfurt. On 19 May 1848 he was elected to the Frankfurt National Assembly and sat with the Casino group (constitutional liberals). He proposed a new Article 1 for the Basic Rights: "Das deutsche Volk ist ein Volk von Freien, und deutscher Boden duldet keine Knechtschaft. Fremde Unfreie, die auf ihm verweilen, macht er frei." It was rejected 192 to 205. He also argued for abolishing the nobility and spoke on Schleswig-Holstein, and he resigned on 2 October 1848. **[sourced]** Freies Deutsches Hochstift exhibition: https://freies-deutsches-hochstift.de/en/mediaguide/romantik-ausstellung/3-obergeschoss/handschriftenstudio/archiv/romantik-und-parlamentarismus/jacob-grimm-artikel-1/ ; Wilhelm Bleek, "Die Brüder Grimm und die deutsche Politik", *Aus Politik und Zeitgeschichte* 1/1986, OA: https://www.bpb.de/shop/zeitschriften/apuz/archiv/533630/die-brueder-grimm-und-die-deutsche-politik/
- Bleek stresses that the Grimms' nationalism was cultural and linguistic, not expansionist, and that the Nazis later distorted it.

## 3. How the tales changed, 1812 to 1857: documented examples

The editions are 1812/15 (1st), 1819 (2nd), 1837 (3rd), 1840 (4th), 1843 (5th), 1850 (6th) and 1857 (7th). There are also ten "Kleine Ausgaben" of 50 tales, the first in 1825 with plates by Ludwig Emil Grimm. Wilhelm did most of the revising.

| Tale | 1812/15 | Later | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sneewittchen** (KHM 53) | The persecutor is Snow White's own mother. The queen bears the child, then becomes jealous of her beauty. Ending: the queen dances to death in red-hot iron slippers. | **1819:** "Und wie das Kind geboren war, starb die Königin. Ueber ein Jahr nahm sich der König eine andere Gemahlin". From here on she is the **Stiefmutter**, called "gottlose Stiefmutter" at the wedding. The iron-shoe ending is kept. | **[verified]** Wikisource 1812 and 1819 |
| **Hänsel und Gretel** (KHM 15) | "die Mutter" throughout (11 occurrences). | Still "Mutter" in 1819. **1840:** "Stiefmutter" (4×). **1857:** mostly "die Frau"; Stiefmutter 1×, a residual "Mutter" 2×. The 1857 text is about 25% longer and more elaborated. | **[verified]** Wikisource 1812, 1819, 1840, 1857 |
| **Rapunzel** (KHM 12) | Rapunzel betrays the prince's visits by asking why "meine Kleiderchen werden mir so eng und wollen nicht mehr passen". She later bears twins. | **1819:** the tight-clothes line becomes "sie wird mir viel schwerer heraufzuziehen als der junge König[ssohn]", so the pregnancy is no longer the giveaway, though the twins remain. **1857** adds that the prince asked "ob sie ihn zum Manne nehmen wollte" and she said yes, an implied marriage. | **[verified]** Wikisource 1812, 1819, 1857 |
| **Aschenputtel** (KHM 21) | The stepsisters cut off heel and toes. Ending: doves announce the true bride and the sisters turn pale. No blinding. | **1819:** added ending in which the doves peck out one eye of each sister on the way into church and the other on the way out. | **[verified]** Wikisource 1812, 1819. Violence *added* (cf. Tatar) |
| **"Wie Kinder Schlachtens miteinander gespielt haben"** (1812 KHM 22) | Children play at butchering and one kills another. | Dropped after 1812. | **[sourced]** Zipes 2014; Tatar |
| **Der gestiefelte Kater, Blaubart** (1812 KHM 33, 62) and others | Included in 1812. | Dropped in 1819 as too close to French (Perrault) literary versions. | **[sourced]** Zipes 2014; Uther, *Handbuch* |
| **Der Jude im Dorn** (1815 KHM 24, later KHM 110) | Antisemitic tale already present in 1815. | Wilhelm's revisions make the Jewish figure more negative, e.g. the 1837 description of a Jew with a long goatee called a "Schelm/Bösewicht". It was included in the *Kleine Ausgabe* for children. | **[sourced]** Helfer 2011; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jew_Among_Thorns |

**Christianisation.** Scholars (Zipes, Tatar, Bottigheimer, Uther) say Wilhelm increased pious formulas and Christian framing over the editions. The ten *Kinderlegenden* were added in 1819, and phrases such as "der liebe Gott wird uns schon helfen" appear. Caution: a word count in Hänsel und Gretel shows "Gott" already 3× in 1812 and 3× in 1857. The shift is real across the corpus, but you should check per tale before using it as a game example. **[verified for H&G only]**

**Ölenberg manuscript (1810) vs 1812.** Many tales are much terser in the manuscript. It is widely reported that in the manuscript Snow White's *father* finds her coffin and has physicians revive her. **[sourced, not checked against Rölleke 1975]**

**Sources for this section:** German Wikisource has transcriptions with page scans of every edition: https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Kinder-_und_Hausm%C3%A4rchen (page titles follow the pattern "Sneewittchen (1819)", "Hänsel und Grethel (1840)"). Also Tatar, *Hard Facts*; Zipes 2014 (introduction); Uther, *Handbuch*; Rölleke 1975.

## 4. Reception

### 19th century: Romanticism and nationalism
- The 1812 preface presents the tales as remnants of an ancient German "Poesie" to be rescued. The 1815 preface stylises Viehmann as an authentic Hessian peasant narrator. Scholarship since Rölleke treats this framing as partly constructed. The Napoleonic occupation is the context for that cultural nationalism (Bleek; Martus). Kamenetsky (1992) and Zipes (1988/2002) trace how the 19th century made the KHM the German "Hausbuch", second only to the Bible. That description is a common trope **[sourced]**.
- **Illustrators:**
  - Ludwig Emil Grimm, the brothers' younger brother, drew the plates for the 1819 frontispieces and the 1825 *Kleine Ausgabe*. The portrait of Viehmann is his. **[sourced]**
  - Ludwig Richter (1803–1884) is chiefly associated with Ludwig Bechstein's rival *Deutsches Märchenbuch*, illustrated from 1853, though he also made Grimm woodcuts. Richter's Biedermeier imagery shaped the "German fairy-tale" look of both books. Don't credit Richter with illustrating the KHM as such. **[sourced]**
  - Otto Ubbelohde (1867–1922) made about 448 pen drawings, 1906–09, for a 3-volume KHM published by Turm-Verlag, Leipzig, in 1907–09. They are set in real landscapes and buildings around Marburg and Goßfelden in Hesse, which makes them directly useful as Hessian visual reference. The originals are at the Otto-Ubbelohde-Haus, Goßfelden, and his sketchbooks were digitised by UB Marburg in 2021. Ubbelohde died in 1922, so his work is public domain. **[sourced]** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Ubbelohde ; https://www.uni-marburg.de/de/ub/aktuelles/nachrichten/2021/otto-ubbelohdes-skizzenbuecher-digital

### Disney
- ***Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs*** (1937). Changes documented by Zipes ("Breaking the Disney Spell"):
  - The dwarfs, anonymous in Grimm, become named, comic and individualised.
  - The prince appears at the start and drives the queen's jealousy.
  - The queen's three attempts (laces, comb, apple) become one, the apple.
  - Snow White is revived by "love's first kiss", not by the apple piece being jolted loose when a bearer stumbles.
  - The queen dies falling from a cliff in a storm, not in red-hot iron shoes at the wedding.
  **[sourced]**
- Other Disney adaptations of Grimm or Grimm-adjacent tales: *Cinderella* (1950; based mainly on Perrault), *Sleeping Beauty* (1959; Perrault and Tchaikovsky more than "Dornröschen"), *Tangled* (2010; Rapunzel), *Frozen* (2013; Andersen, not Grimm). Zipes's charge is that Disney replaced the tales' variability with a single sanitised, commodified version.

### DEFA (GDR) and West German films
- **Qinna Shen, *The Politics of Magic: DEFA Fairy-Tale Films*.** Wayne State UP, 2015. Shows that DEFA fairy-tale films ranged from dogmatic to quietly subversive. Shen compares them with their print sources and with Nazi, West German and Disney versions. https://muse.jhu.edu/book/40413
- Representative DEFA titles:
  - *Das singende, klingende Bäumchen* (1957)
  - *Schneewittchen* (1961)
  - *Rotkäppchen* (1962, Götz Friedrich)
  - *Frau Holle* (1963)
  - *König Drosselbart* (1965)
  - *Dornröschen* (1971)
  - *Sechse kommen durch die Welt* (1972)
  - *Drei Haselnüsse für Aschenbrödel* (1973, Czech-GDR co-production, based on Božena Němcová)
  DEFA Film Library, UMass Amherst: https://www.umass.edu/defa/
- West Germany in the 1950s: producers such as Fritz Genschow, Hubert Schonger and the Diehl brothers continued their Nazi-era fairy-tale film work almost seamlessly into cheap children's films. Since 2005 TV series have followed: ZDF *Märchenperlen*, ARD *Sechs auf einen Streich* (2008–). Zipes's "Struggle for the Grimms' Throne" covers FRG/GDR competition over the Grimm heritage. **[sourced]**

### Modern retellings (selection)
- Anne Sexton, *Transformations* (1971)
- Angela Carter, *The Bloody Chamber* (1979)
- Sondheim/Lapine, *Into the Woods* (1986/87)

## 5. Nazi appropriation and the post-1945 debate

### Use in NS education and ideology
- Kamenetsky (1972, 1977, 1984) documents the following.
  - The NS state treated the KHM as a source of "Nordic-Germanic" values. Folklore study was steered ideologically, and its international comparative dimension was suppressed.
  - Party-approved editions and school readers used the tales for "völkisch" and racial education.
  - Interpretive literature read tales racially. A frequently cited example is Aschenputtel as the racially pure heroine whom the prince recognises by instinct, with the stepmother as racial alien.
  **[sourced]** Kamenetsky; Tatar, *Annotated Brothers Grimm*.
- The often-quoted NS slogan "Keine deutsche Kindheit ohne Märchen…" circulates in secondary sources. The original source was not located: **[unverified]**.
- Claims that *Hitler personally* praised the tales and that the NS regime wanted a copy in every household come via Tatar and Lynn H. Nicholas (*Cruel World*, 2005). The primary sources were not checked: **[unverified]**.
- Louis L. Snyder, *Roots of German Nationalism* (Indiana UP, 1978), argues that "a large part of the Nazi literature designed for children was merely a modernized version of the Grimms' tales". Treat this as an older, polemical view. **[sourced]**

### NS-era films
- About 20 live-action fairy-tale films were made in 1933–45. The four main producers included Hubert Schonger, Fritz Genschow and the Diehl brothers.
- ***Rotkäppchen und der Wolf*** (1937, Fritz Genschow and Renée Stobrawa) has a black-and-white frame story set in the Nazi present and a colour fairy-tale section, with Genschow as a rescuing "Onkel Jäger". https://f-films.deutsches-filminstitut.de/film/f013195.htm ; https://www.filmportal.de
- A sometimes-repeated claim that an NS Rotkäppchen film cast the wolf as Jewish, or the rescuer as an SS man, is **[unverified]**. Check Schlesinger before using it.
- Studies:
  - Ron Schlesinger, *Rotkäppchen im Dritten Reich. Die deutsche Märchenfilmproduktion zwischen 1933 und 1945* (DEFA-Stiftung research project; year **[unverified]**)
  - Cornelia Anett Endler on the Diehl brothers' Märchenfilme
  - Eric Rentschler, *The Ministry of Illusion* (Harvard UP, 1996), for context

### Antisemitic tales
- **"Der Jude im Dorn"** (KHM 110). Wilhelm's revisions made the Jewish figure more contemptible. Arnold Zweig identified it as antisemitic incitement in 1936. Other KHM tales with antisemitic content: "Der gute Handel" (KHM 7) and "Die klare Sonne bringt's an den Tag" (KHM 115). Antisemitic legends in the *Deutsche Sagen*: "Der Judenstein", "Das von den Juden getötete Mägdlein". **[sourced]** Helfer 2011; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jew_Among_Thorns ; text: https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Der_Jude_im_Dorn
- Article "Das Bild des Juden in den Märchen der Brüder Grimm", *Zeitschrift für Religions- und Geistesgeschichte* 63, no. 3 (2011): 234–249. Author name **[unverified]**: PhilPapers record https://philpapers.org/rec/BOCDBD (blocked). It reports that of 56 tales examined, Jews appear mostly as usurers, traders, thieves or murderers; 2 are positive and 7 neutral. **[sourced via abstract snippet]**
- *Game guidance:* scholars disagree whether these texts mainly reflect the Grimms' own views or the popular prejudice they recorded. Helfer stresses Wilhelm's active sharpening of the portrayal. Any treatment should present the antisemitism as a documented feature of the corpus and its revisions, not leave it out silently and not reproduce it.

### Post-1945: "the Allies banned Grimm". What is actually documented
- **Documented (scholarly):**
  - Hermann Bausinger (1976, OA, Tübingen) refers to a postwar campaign against the "Grimm'sche Märchengreuel", "teilweise von Militärregierungen gesteuert" (partly steered by military governments), which looked for the roots of Nazi brutality in the tales' cruelty. http://hdl.handle.net/10900/47615
  - A British officer, Major T. J. Leonard, argued in 1947 (German Wikipedia gives *First Steps in Cruelty*) that the tales trained German children in "all the varieties of barbarousness" and so "the role of the hangman". **[sourced]** The original publication was not located; venue and title **[unverified]**.
  - Bastian (1981) and Zipes (1988/2002, "Struggle for the Grimms' Throne") cover the West German pedagogical "Märchenstreit" that followed. Fairy-tale-critical views dominated until the 1970s, and Bettelheim (1976) helped turn the debate.
- **Claimed with varying details (secondary, primary not found):**
  - Kamenetsky (as summarised in course materials) says that "in 1945 the Allied Forces briefly banned the publication of the Grimm tales".
  - A *TES* article (Cameron Morrell, 26 Jan 1996) says "in 1946 Britain banned publication of the tales within its occupation zone": https://www.tes.com/news/banned-grimm-tales-survive-censors
  - English Wikipedia repeats a ban "in the British Occupation Zone", citing only that *TES* piece.
  - German Wikipedia says the KHM were weeded out of schools and libraries in the US zone and "shipped overseas", and that the British zone issued no reprint licence "for a time". Its footnotes could not be read in this session.
  - The versions disagree on year (1945/46), on which powers, and on whether this was a ban, a refusal of licences, or library weeding.
- **Counter-evidence:** the Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin's exhibition on Berlin children's books 1945–1950 lists a *Grimms Märchen* with paper-cut illustrations (Groszer, **1945**) as "the first children's book published in Berlin after the war". It also lists Grimm single-tale editions from 1946–50, including one published in the **French** sector in 1946 and one from Volk und Wissen (Soviet sector) in 1948. https://staatsbibliothek-berlin.de/die-staatsbibliothek/abteilungen/kinder-und-jugendbuecher/aktuelles/ausstellung/berliner-ausgaben/1945-bis-1950
- **Assessment:**
  - A general, all-Allied ban on the Grimm tales across occupied Germany is **false** as usually stated.
  - A temporary British-zone licensing hold, and removals from US-zone schools and libraries, are **plausible but unverified at the level of primary documents** (orders, licence files).
  - Inference, not a sourced finding: all publishing in 1945–49 needed a military-government licence, and Nazi-era school readers were withdrawn wholesale. Restrictions on NS-era Grimm schoolbook editions may have been remembered as a "ban on Grimm".
  - For the game, say "occupation authorities, especially the British, viewed the tales with suspicion and briefly restricted them", not "the Allies banned Grimm". To settle it, consult Bastian 1981 and Zipes's "Struggle" chapter, and check British Control Commission (FO 1050/1056) or OMGUS records.

1. Take Wikisource or DTA texts for 1812, 1819 and 1857 per tale.
2. Diff the editions automatically. This gives concrete "the text changed here" content for gameplay.

## 7. Myths and claims register (for writers)

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| The Grimms collected tales from illiterate peasants. | **Largely false.** Most informants were young, literate, middle-class women from Kassel families (Wild, Hassenpflug, Haxthausen); several were of Huguenot descent. Viehmann was a tailor's wife and innkeeper's daughter of Huguenot descent who sold produce, not a stereotypical peasant. (Rölleke; Ellis) |
| "Die alte Marie" was an old housekeeper. | **False.** It was Marie Hassenpflug (Rölleke). |
| Mothers became stepmothers. | **True.** Snow White in 1819; Hänsel und Gretel in 1840. **[verified]** |
| The Grimms removed all violence. | **False.** Some violence was added, e.g. the blinding in Aschenputtel (1819). **[verified]** |
| Rapunzel's pregnancy was erased. | **Half-true.** The tight-dress clue was removed in 1819, but the twins remain in every edition. **[verified]** |
| Jacob died at "Frucht"; the DWB was completed in 1961. | **True** (completed January 1961; source volume 1971). |
| Grimm discovered Grimm's law. | **Misleading.** Rask published the correspondences first (1818). Grimm systematised them, including the High German shift (1822). |
| The Allies banned Grimm's fairy tales after 1945. | **False as usually stated; partially documented.** A suspicion campaign happened, and temporary zonal restrictions are plausible. Grimm editions appeared in Berlin from 1945, including in the French sector. |
| Hitler personally praised the tales / every household had to own one. | **[unverified]** |
| Disney's Snow White follows Grimm. | **False** in key plot points (Zipes). |
